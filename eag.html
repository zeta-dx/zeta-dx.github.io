What is HbA1c?

HbA1c, also called A1C or glycated hemoglobin, is a laboratory measurement that reflects average blood glucose exposure over approximately the previous two to three months.

Glucose circulating in the bloodstream can attach to hemoglobin within red blood cells. The higher the average blood glucose concentration, the greater the proportion of hemoglobin that becomes glycated.

Because red blood cells circulate for approximately three months, HbA1c provides a longer-term picture of blood glucose than a single blood glucose measurement.

Why is HbA1c useful?

  • It can be used as one of the tests for diagnosing prediabetes and diabetes.
  • It provides an indication of average glucose over the previous 2–3 months.
  • It can be used to monitor diabetes management.
  • It provides a longer-term perspective than an isolated finger-stick glucose measurement.

The ADA notes that A1C does not require fasting, which is one of its practical advantages as a test.

What is Estimated Average Glucose (eAG)?

Estimated average glucose, or eAG, converts an HbA1c percentage into an approximate average glucose concentration expressed in familiar glucose units such as mg/dL or mmol/L.

For example, an HbA1c of 7.0% corresponds to an eAG of approximately 154 mg/dL, or approximately 8.6 mmol/L.

How is eAG calculated?

eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7

For conversion to mmol/L:

eAG (mmol/L) = eAG (mg/dL) ÷ 18

The reverse relationship can be written as:

HbA1c (%) = [eAG (mg/dL) + 46.7] ÷ 28.7
The eAG equation describes a population-derived relationship between HbA1c and average glucose. It is an estimate and should not be interpreted as an exact prediction of an individual's glucose readings.

HbA1c vs eAG

HbA1c eAG What it represents
HbA1c % Percentage of hemoglobin that is glycated
eAG mg/dL or mmol/L Estimated average glucose corresponding to the HbA1c

The two values describe related information in different ways. HbA1c is the laboratory measurement, while eAG expresses that result using glucose units that are more familiar from blood glucose meters.

What is FBS / FPG?

FBS is commonly used to mean fasting blood sugar. In laboratory and ADA terminology, the corresponding term is usually fasting plasma glucose (FPG).

Fasting means that the person has had no caloric intake for at least 8 hours. Water is generally permitted during the fasting period.

Why is fasting glucose measured?

Fasting glucose provides a measurement of blood glucose under standardized fasting conditions. It is one of the tests that can be used to identify prediabetes and diabetes.

Fasting Plasma Glucose ADA category
<100 mg/dL Normal
100–125 mg/dL Prediabetes
≥126 mg/dL Diabetes range

An abnormal diagnostic result generally requires appropriate confirmation unless there is unequivocal hyperglycemia or another qualifying clinical situation.

What is PBS / Postprandial Blood Glucose?

PBS is commonly used to describe postprandial blood sugar. The ADA terminology is postprandial blood glucose (PPG).

Postprandial glucose refers to the blood glucose measurement taken after eating. For diabetes management, the ADA commonly discusses postprandial glucose measurement at 1–2 hours after the beginning of a meal.

Why is postprandial glucose useful?

Postprandial glucose helps show how blood glucose responds to food and other factors around meals. It can provide information that may not be apparent from a fasting measurement alone.

ADA target for many nonpregnant adults with diabetes

1–2 hours after beginning a meal:
<180 mg/dL

Glucose targets are individualized. The ADA notes that different targets may be appropriate depending on factors such as age, duration of diabetes, comorbidities, cardiovascular disease, complications and risk of hypoglycemia.

HbA1c, FPG and Postprandial Glucose: What is the Difference?

Test Time represented Main purpose
HbA1c Approximately 2–3 months Long-term glycemic exposure
FPG / FBS Current fasting state Fasting glucose assessment
PPG / PBS 1–2 hours after beginning a meal Post-meal glucose response

These measurements should not be expected to give identical information. HbA1c provides a long-term average, while FPG and PPG represent glucose at specific points in time.

What is Diabetes?

Diabetes is a group of conditions characterized by elevated blood glucose levels. Glucose is an important source of energy, and insulin plays a major role in helping glucose move from the bloodstream into cells.

In diabetes, the body may not produce enough insulin, may not use insulin effectively, or both. The resulting elevation in blood glucose can persist over time.

Major types of diabetes

  • Type 1 diabetes: an autoimmune condition in which the body's immune system attacks insulin-producing beta cells.
  • Type 2 diabetes: the most common form, generally involving insulin resistance together with progressive impairment of insulin secretion.
  • Gestational diabetes: diabetes diagnosed during pregnancy.

Diabetes can affect multiple organs over time. Persistent hyperglycemia is associated with complications involving the eyes, kidneys, nerves, cardiovascular system and other tissues.

ADA Criteria for Diabetes and Prediabetes

The ADA recognizes several laboratory approaches for diagnosing diabetes. The appropriate test depends on the clinical situation.

Test Normal Prediabetes Diabetes
HbA1c <5.7% 5.7–6.4% ≥6.5%
Fasting Plasma Glucose <100 mg/dL 100–125 mg/dL ≥126 mg/dL
2-hour OGTT <140 mg/dL 140–199 mg/dL ≥200 mg/dL

Random Plasma Glucose

A random plasma glucose of ≥200 mg/dL can be diagnostic of diabetes when accompanied by classic symptoms of hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis.

A diagnostic threshold should not be interpreted as a standalone self-diagnosis. ADA notes that, in the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, abnormal diagnostic testing generally requires confirmation.

HbA1c Targets in Diabetes

For many nonpregnant adults with diabetes, the ADA lists an HbA1c target of <7%. However, the appropriate target is individualized.

The ADA emphasizes that more or less stringent goals may be appropriate depending on factors such as age, life expectancy, duration of diabetes, comorbidities, cardiovascular disease, complications and risk of hypoglycemia.

<7%

Corresponding eAG:
<154 mg/dL

A target should therefore not be interpreted as a universal value for every person with diabetes.

Why Might HbA1c and Glucose Readings Not Match?

The eAG derived from HbA1c is a statistical estimate. A person's actual glucose measurements can vary considerably around that average.

For example, two people can have the same HbA1c but different patterns of fasting glucose, post-meal glucose and glucose variability.

Factors that can affect HbA1c interpretation

  • Conditions that alter red blood cell lifespan.
  • Some forms of anemia.
  • Recent blood loss or transfusion.
  • Hemoglobin variants.
  • Certain medical conditions or treatments that affect red blood cell turnover.

When HbA1c appears inconsistent with measured glucose, the result should be evaluated in the appropriate clinical and laboratory context.

Important Information

Educational use only. This calculator provides an estimated relationship between HbA1c and average glucose. It is not intended to diagnose diabetes or determine an individual's treatment.

Blood glucose results can vary because of meals, physical activity, illness, stress, medications and many other factors. A single glucose measurement should therefore not automatically be interpreted as a person's long-term average glucose.

For diagnosis or treatment decisions, laboratory results should be interpreted by an appropriately qualified healthcare professional.
