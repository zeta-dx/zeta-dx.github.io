What is eGFR?

Estimated glomerular filtration rate, commonly called eGFR, is an estimate of how efficiently the kidneys filter blood. Glomerular filtration rate is considered the best overall index of kidney function.

GFR cannot normally be measured directly using a routine blood test. Instead, estimating equations use biomarkers found in the blood, primarily serum creatinine and, when available, cystatin C, together with age and sex.

eGFR is usually reported in mL/min/1.73 m². The 1.73 m² represents a standard body-surface-area reference.

The adult equations used on this page are the race-free CKD-EPI equations recommended by the National Kidney Foundation and American Society of Nephrology Task Force.

Why is eGFR measured?

eGFR is primarily used to assess kidney filtration and to help identify and monitor chronic kidney disease. It can also help clinicians understand changes in kidney function over time.

Common uses include:

  • Assessing kidney filtration.
  • Detecting and monitoring chronic kidney disease.
  • Monitoring changes in kidney function over time.
  • Supporting assessment of patients at risk of kidney disease.
  • Providing information that may be considered in medication-related decisions.

eGFR should be interpreted together with the clinical situation, previous results and other laboratory findings rather than being treated as an isolated number.

How is eGFR calculated?

Modern adult eGFR equations were developed by comparing measured GFR with filtration biomarkers and demographic variables. The current race-free CKD-EPI equations use serum creatinine, cystatin C, age and sex depending on the equation selected.

2021 CKD-EPI Creatinine Equation

eGFR = 142 × min(SCr/κ,1)α × max(SCr/κ,1)−1.200 × 0.9938Age × 1.012 [if female]

Where SCr is standardized serum creatinine in mg/dL, κ is 0.7 for females and 0.9 for males, and α is −0.241 for females and −0.302 for males.

2021 CKD-EPI Creatinine-Cystatin C Equation

eGFR = 135 × min(SCr/κ,1)α × max(SCr/κ,1)−0.544 × min(Scys/0.8,1)−0.323 × max(Scys/0.8,1)−0.778 × 0.9961Age × 0.963 [if female]

Here Scys represents standardized serum cystatin C in mg/L. The α value is −0.219 for females and −0.144 for males.

2012 CKD-EPI Cystatin C Equation

eGFR = 133 × min(Scys/0.8,1)−0.499 × max(Scys/0.8,1)−1.328 × 0.996Age × 0.932 [if female]

This equation uses age, sex and standardized serum cystatin C without serum creatinine.

NIDDK specifies standardized creatinine measurements traceable to an IDMS reference procedure and standardized cystatin C measurements traceable to the appropriate IFCC reference material for these equations.

Understanding Serum Creatinine

Creatinine is a waste product produced primarily from normal muscle metabolism. It enters the bloodstream and is eliminated mainly through kidney filtration.

Because serum creatinine is influenced by kidney filtration, it is one of the most widely used biomarkers for estimating GFR.

Why is creatinine not a perfect measure of GFR?

Creatinine production is affected by factors such as muscle mass, age and dietary intake. Consequently, two people with the same GFR can potentially have different serum creatinine concentrations.

This is why eGFR equations combine creatinine with demographic variables rather than interpreting serum creatinine alone.

For the 2021 CKD-EPI equation: The serum creatinine measurement should be standardized to an IDMS-traceable method.

Understanding Cystatin C

Cystatin C is a small protein produced by cells throughout the body. It is freely filtered by the glomerulus and is therefore useful as an endogenous filtration marker.

Cystatin C is less dependent on muscle mass than creatinine, which provides a useful alternative biomarker when creatinine may be affected by non-GFR-related factors.

Why use creatinine and cystatin C together?

Creatinine and cystatin C have different non-GFR determinants. Combining the two biomarkers can therefore reduce some of the limitations associated with relying on either biomarker alone.

NIDDK states that the combined creatinine-cystatin C equation generally provides greater accuracy than serum creatinine alone.

Cystatin C should be measured using an assay appropriate for use with the standardized CKD-EPI cystatin C equation.

Understanding Albumin

Albumin is the most abundant protein in plasma. It is produced primarily by the liver and has important roles in maintaining plasma oncotic pressure and transporting various substances through the bloodstream.

Under normal conditions, only a small amount of albumin passes into urine because the kidney filtration barrier restricts the passage of larger proteins.

Increased urinary albumin can therefore be an important marker of kidney damage, even when eGFR is not markedly reduced.

Albumin-Creatinine Ratio (ACR)

The urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, or uACR, relates urinary albumin concentration to urinary creatinine concentration. This helps account for variation in urine concentration.

ACR does not enter the CKD-EPI eGFR equation. Instead, it provides complementary information about kidney damage and is considered alongside eGFR.

Category ACR Description
A1 <30 mg/g Normal to mildly increased
A2 30–300 mg/g Moderately increased
A3 >300 mg/g Severely increased

eGFR / GFR Categories

Kidney function is commonly divided into GFR categories. These categories describe the level of estimated filtration.

Category eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) Description
G1 ≥90 Normal or high
G2 60–89 Mildly decreased
G3a 45–59 Mildly to moderately decreased
G3b 30–44 Moderately to severely decreased
G4 15–29 Severely decreased
G5 <15 Kidney failure
In the absence of evidence of kidney damage, G1 and G2 alone do not fulfill the criteria for CKD.

Why eGFR and ACR Are Used Together

Kidney disease assessment is more informative when filtration and markers of kidney damage are considered together.

eGFR describes the estimated level of kidney filtration, while albuminuria provides information about damage to the kidney filtration barrier.

This is sometimes described as the CGA approach: Cause, GFR category and Albuminuria category.

Component What it describes
G category Level of kidney filtration
A category Level of albuminuria
Cause Underlying cause of kidney disease

Neither eGFR nor ACR should be interpreted as a diagnosis in isolation. Persistent abnormalities over time and clinical context are important.

Limitations of eGFR

eGFR is an estimate and is not a direct measurement of GFR. All estimating equations have limitations.

  • eGFR becomes less precise at higher levels of GFR.
  • Serum creatinine can be affected by muscle mass, diet and other non-GFR factors.
  • Cystatin C can also be influenced by factors unrelated to filtration.
  • A single eGFR value may be less informative than the trend over time.
  • Acute changes in kidney function can make estimating equations less reliable because they assume relatively stable biomarker concentrations.
  • Different equations should not automatically be interpreted as equivalent measurements.
